# Adaptation Doctrine
**How the house style travels without taking over the subject**

## 1. The governing rule

**The source material governs identity. The house style governs presentation.**

The Codex is reusable because it does not require every project to depict the same fictional world. It establishes a shared visual temperament: material age, deliberate hierarchy, textured restraint, historical weight, readable composition, and hard-edged pixel construction where the project calls for it.

The style is not a lore generator. It is not permission to add aristocrats, abbeys, divine-right symbolism, or broken chains to every project.

## 2. Separate subject from treatment

For every new project, identify two layers before producing work.

### Subject layer — supplied by the project
- Game premise and rules.
- Character identity, age, appearance, personality, history, and relationships.
- Setting, period, geography, social structures, and factual references.
- Objects, factions, institutions, and narrative events.
- Functional interface requirements and accessibility needs.

### Treatment layer — supplied by the Codex
- Visual mood and material vocabulary.
- Palette discipline and pixel-art construction.
- Typography and ornament.
- Composition, value hierarchy, and lighting.
- Tone of copy and labels.
- Rules for negative space, borders, and decorative detail.

Never allow a treatment choice to overwrite a subject fact.

## 3. Adapt by project type

### Narrative game
Use the estate's register as an atmospheric and compositional standard. Characters and locations must come from the narrative source. A character's clothing, cultural background, silhouette, and personal history should not be aristocratized unless the source says so.

### Card game, solitaire, or tabletop adaptation
The subject is the game mechanic. Apply the Codex through:
- Card backs and suit treatments.
- Framed playing surfaces.
- Typography, score and status labels.
- Parchment, stone, oxblood, and restrained gilt.
- Menu, pause screen, settings, and help presentation.

Do not force abbeys, castles, or lore panels into a solitaire game that does not need them. A clean card table can carry the house style without pretending to be a medieval campaign.

### Utility or puzzle game
Preserve clarity and interaction speed. Use the house typography, material borders, color discipline, and visual restraint. Ornament must not obscure the board, controls, state, or feedback.

### Website or digital codex
Think in terms of an annotated archive, field guide, or private library. Use editorial hierarchy, persistent navigation, readable long-form text, and meaningful ornament. Avoid dashboard conventions unless the subject truly requires a dashboard.

### Character-driven experience
Character canon takes priority over aesthetic archetypes. A plain-dressed, modern, comic, or otherwise unexpected character can belong in the house style. Translate their existing identity through framing, palette, texture, and pixel construction rather than changing who they are.

## 4. Register selection

Select one leading register for each asset and state why.

| Register | Use when | Do not use when |
|---|---|---|
| Estate of the Crown | Formal identity, interface, portrait presentation, order, hierarchy | A scene needs devotional decay or philosophical abstraction |
| The Last Abbey | Memory, ruined institutions, lore environments, sacred or historical melancholy | The asset is a functional board where detail would obstruct play |
| The Broken Measure / Rootbound | Essays, symbolic objects, philosophical tension, nature against systems | The subject has no reason to carry symbolic weight |

The registers can mix, but mixing must be intentional. One should lead.

## 5. Subject-specific adaptation sequence

1. Read the supplied brief, lore bible, and physical descriptions.
2. Extract facts without altering them.
3. Identify the project's functional requirements.
4. Select the leading Codex register.
5. Translate the source into material, palette, composition, and typography decisions.
6. Add only details that are consistent with the source and explicitly label inventions.
7. Test the result at intended display size.
8. Check readability and mechanical usability.
9. Record any deliberate exception to the house standard.

## 6. The "Todd's Solitaire" test

A project called *Todd's Solitaire* is a valid test of the doctrine.

It should not automatically become a medieval estate-management game. It may simply be a solitaire game with:
- A dark, restrained playing surface.
- Aged card stock and crisp card faces.
- Heraldic backs or a restrained geometric device.
- Oxblood selection states.
- Antique-gold frame details.
- EB Garamond for readable card labels, with small Press Start 2P utility labels where legible.
- No decorative animation that compromises card handling.

The game rules and card readability remain primary. The Codex informs the atmosphere rather than hijacking the subject.

## 7. Adaptation review

Ask:

- Does the project remain recognizably itself?
- Have I imported any lore, class structure, period, or symbols the source did not establish?
- Is the visual register appropriate to the subject and interface?
- Does the art preserve its functional silhouette and readable state?
- Is ornament doing real work?
- Are all deviations documented rather than silently normalized?

## 8. When the source and house style conflict

The source wins on identity and factual content. Functional requirements win on usability. The Codex wins on presentation only where it does not contradict either.

If a conflict is material, state it explicitly and offer a treatment that preserves the subject. Do not quietly rewrite the subject to make it fit the house.
