# Visual Constitution
**The Estate Under Siege · House Standard · Canonical reference**

## 1. Purpose

The Estate Codex is a permanent creative standard for work across different games and media. It is not a fictional setting that every project must inhabit. It is a visual, material, and tonal identity that can be applied to different subjects without flattening their individual character.

The governing distinction is:

> **The source material governs identity. The house style governs presentation.**

A character bible decides who a character is. A physical description decides what they look like. A game's rules decide what its interface must do. The Codex decides how these things should feel and how they should be visually expressed.

## 2. The central image: an order under pressure

The Estate Under Siege depicts a functioning old order that has not yet become a ruin and has not secured a victory. It is inhabited, governed, maintained, and cultivated. Its architecture communicates authority and inherited duty. Its visible wear communicates pressure.

The estate must look worth preserving. Its vulnerability is what gives it meaning.

This tension governs every register:

- **Dignity without triumphalism.**
- **Age without abandonment.**
- **Order without sterility.**
- **Decay without horror.**
- **Mystery without occult cliché.**
- **Ornament without indiscriminate clutter.**

## 3. Intellectual and artistic vocabulary

The house style draws from three historical registers:

### Romanticism
The sublime scale of landscape, weather, age, distance, and human vulnerability. Nature should feel older than any one institution. Light is selective and meaningful; the world is not evenly illuminated for the convenience of a brochure.

### Gothic Revival
Vertical emphasis, pointed arches, tracery, deep recesses, carved stone, devotional melancholy, and the physical memory of institutions. Gothic forms may be intact, damaged, or reclaimed by vegetation.

### Neoclassicism
Proportion, axial composition, columns, pediments, formal gardens, statuary, symmetry, and the material language of public authority. Neoclassical order provides the estate's structural confidence; weathering and asymmetry prevent it from becoming sterile.

The Organic Society is an underlying worldview, not a checklist of props. It is suggested through relationships between manor, chapel, village, cultivated land, inherited structures, and distinct forms of work. The image should imply a whole inhabited society, not merely a rich person's building.

## 4. Direction ledger

The three registers are complementary. They are not three competing themes.

### B leads: Estate of the Crown
**Primary identity, homepage, general UI, portraits, heraldry, and formal presentation.**

- Monumental manor and landed estate.
- Central axes and deliberate hierarchy.
- Classical proportions, columns, pediments, formal grounds.
- Aged heraldry, gilt frames, stone plates, oxblood and parchment.
- Dignity under strain rather than royal celebration.

### A supports: The Last Abbey
**Lore scenes, environmental dividers, institutional memory, and devotional melancholy.**

- Ruined or neglected Gothic interiors.
- Ancient oak roots dividing old foundations.
- Broken rose windows, cloisters, moss, dust, mist.
- Narrow light, deep recesses, aged statuary.
- The persistence of memory after the loss of certainty.

### C accents: The Broken Measure / Rootbound
**Essays, philosophical passages, symbolic objects, and moments of intellectual rupture.**

- Storm-dark ridges and ancient sanctuaries.
- Broken sundials, compasses, measuring instruments, and chains.
- Woodcut-block or checkerboard dithering.
- Selective pale light against large dark masses.
- Symbolism rooted in bronze, stone, parchment, and iron.

## 5. The three failure modes to resist

### A. Generic corporate fantasy
Reject glossy gradients, polished magical castles, generic medieval iconography, glowing runes, and interchangeable fantasy UI. The work must feel materially specific: limestone, iron, worn parchment, bronze, oak, carved stone, and deliberate pixel clusters.

### B. Polished but sterile estate
This is the primary visual risk. Perfect symmetry, immaculate gardens, uniformly illuminated windows, bright gilt, and pristine stone can turn the estate into a celebratory country-house advertisement.

Correct it through visible but restrained contest:
- One neglected wing, not a completely ruined manor.
- Uneven maintenance, weathered stone, damaged cornice, and one or two dark windows.
- Roots lifting paving and beginning to split the oldest foundations.
- A displaced balustrade stone or weathered statue.
- A broken chain partially caught at the garden's edge.
- Storm clouds gathering, not a world-ending tempest.

Do not solve sterility by making everything black and ruined. The subject is preservation under pressure.

### C. Overloaded ornament
Ornament is required when it carries the old-world material language. It is not permission to obscure hierarchy. Use heraldic borders, filigree corners, broken-chain dividers, and carved frames to distinguish sections and important content. Do not decorate every pixel equally.

## 6. Palette and material doctrine

Use only the approved Genesis palette banks in `docs/color-ramp.md` for assets claimed to follow the hardware specification.

Locked color families:
- Imperial midnight and storm navy.
- Tyrian and oxblood purple.
- Warm limestone and aged stone.
- Parchment ivory and bone.
- Antique gold and burnished bronze.
- Deep evergreen and moss.
- Charcoal and cold iron grey.
- Mystic violet.
- One restricted candle amber.

Gold is an accent, not a fill color for the entire estate. Amber is a rare point of human warmth. Cold shadows should dominate the hero environment. Parchment is for reading surfaces, not a reason to make the whole website cream-colored.

Materials should remain distinguishable at native pixel scale. Limestone is not parchment; iron is not charcoal; moss is not evergreen; gilt is not yellow paint.

## 7. Composition rules

- Establish a readable large silhouette before adding architectural detail.
- Use a strong central axis for the Estate of the Crown register, then introduce asymmetry through selective neglect and environmental pressure.
- Preserve negative space where text must sit.
- Let tree, roofline, stone, and sky separate by value before hue.
- Use depth through layered silhouettes, palette shifts, and clustered detail, never blur.
- Put the strongest warm light where it has narrative meaning.
- Keep the broken chain discoverable rather than turning it into a logo pasted into every scene.

## 8. Website and interface tone

This is a codex, not a dashboard.

Required language:
- Intricate Gothic and Neoclassical ornament where it establishes hierarchy.
- Heraldic frames and stone or parchment plates.
- Broken-chain dividers used sparingly.
- Mixed typography: **Cinzel Decorative** headings, **EB Garamond** body text, **Press Start 2P** micro-labels.
- Dense but legible editorial structure.
- Hard borders, square corners, restrained oxblood controls, bone text, moss interaction states.
- Pixel rendering for pixel assets.

The interface should feel like an annotated archive or a carefully maintained private library. It must remain usable. Ornament serves navigation, grouping, emphasis, or material character; it is not decorative noise.

## 9. Hard bans

- Purple/blue SaaS gradients, gradient orbs, glassmorphism, frosted panels, floating rounded shells.
- Fake metric cards, fake charts, generic SaaS filler copy, or generic three-column feature cards as the default page structure.
- "Empower", "unlock", or "elevate" marketing language.
- Generic eyebrow labels used as decoration.
- Bounce/spring animation or scale-on-hover.
- Cyan-on-dark-blue "premium dark mode".
- Emoji icons as interface ornament.
- Generic fantasy castles, steampunk devices, horror/gore, occult clichés, spell effects, modern or sci-fi props.
- Anti-aliased pixel art, blur, smooth gradients, painterly texture, photorealism, 3D rendering, or high-resolution illustration with a pixel filter.
- Anime or chibi treatment unless a future project explicitly requests a separate adaptation and records that exception.

## 10. What may adapt

A new project may change:
- Subject matter, fictional world, character canon, mechanics, content hierarchy.
- Which visual register leads.
- The amount of ornament appropriate to the screen.
- Asset dimensions and engine-specific technical requirements.

A new project may not silently change:
- The source-material hierarchy.
- The difference between identity and presentation.
- The hard bans.
- The material vocabulary and tonal tension.
- The requirement to state technical exceptions honestly.

## 11. Review test

Before accepting a design, ask:

1. Does the subject still look like itself, rather than a generic estate-themed reskin?
2. Does the material language feel aged, tactile, and specific?
3. Is the old order visibly under pressure without being reduced to rubble?
4. Does the composition read at its intended size?
5. Does ornament clarify hierarchy?
6. Are the palette and pixel constraints genuinely observed?
7. Could another AI reproduce the intended direction from the written rules alone?

If the answer to any of these is no, revise the asset or document the intentional exception.
